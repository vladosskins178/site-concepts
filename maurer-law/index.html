About Josh

From Ellensburg to Spokane’s courtrooms.

Joshua Maurer was born and raised in Ellensburg, Washington. When his brother and cousin were in a catastrophic crash with a drunk driver, he saw how one person’s negligence can change another’s life — and decided to pursue personal injury law.

He put himself through Central Washington University and Gonzaga Law School (2007), working at the Spokane County Prosecutor’s Office and a personal injury firm along the way, and opened Maurer Law in 2011.

Josh is an adjunct professor of International Law in Whitworth University’s MBA program, volunteers at the Sinto Senior Center, and coaches his son’s basketball and baseball teams. He lives in Spokane with his wife and children.

  • Nearly two decades of litigation experience

Questions

Before you call

How much are your fees?

Maurer Law takes cases on a contingency fee basis. The usual and customary fee for a settlement is 33.3%.

Do I have to pay if we don’t win?

No. Legal fees are contingent on a successful prosecution of your claim. If your claim is not successful, you will not be charged a fee.

How will I be kept informed about my case?

Every case accepted by Maurer Law is assigned to a staff member who periodically contacts you with updates — and you are always welcome to call the office with questions or concerns.

How long do I have to file a claim in Washington?

Most personal injury and wrongful death claims must be filed within three years of the injury or death (RCW 4.16.080). Claims against government entities require a tort claim notice and a 60-day wait before suit (RCW 4.96.020), and exceptions apply for minors. Early legal review protects your rights and preserves evidence.

How long does a case take?

Straightforward injury cases may resolve within several months. Complex matters — semi-truck collisions, government entities, police misconduct or wrongful death — often take one to three years or longer when litigation is required.

Do I have a civil rights case against the police or a city?

Possibly. Claims can arise from police shootings, excessive force during an arrest, in-custody deaths or serious injuries, and failure to intervene — usually under federal law (42 U.S.C. § 1983) and sometimes Washington law. Not every negative encounter gives rise to a claim, so each case needs careful review.
